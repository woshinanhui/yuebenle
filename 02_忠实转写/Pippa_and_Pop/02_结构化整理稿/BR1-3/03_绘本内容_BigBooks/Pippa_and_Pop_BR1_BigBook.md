## 附录：How to Use the Big Book（教师使用说明）

> 来源：第 48–49 页。内容为原书教学指导与每单元通用理解问题。

**How to use the Big Book**

There is something magical about Big Books; they capture the imagination of children and bring the stories from the Pupil's Book to life in your classroom. Big Books can be read time and again, as young children love familiar stories. They will grow to know and love the characters and share their adventures as you turn the pages. The Big Books in Pippa and Pop contain the nine literacy stories from the Pupil's Book. An animated version of each story is also available on Presentation Plus.

Reading in class should be a shared experience. Reading together with the Big Book mimics the experience many children have at home, listening to stories in their first language. It is an important opportunity to help children develop early-literacy skills, by exposing them to the joy of books, helping them become familiar with reading conventions and by encouraging them to interact with and respond to stories.

While you read the Big Book, children should be engaged in the storytelling and taking part ('living' the story). Through interacting with the story, children develop essential oral language skills. They learn to concentrate and follow a plot, to have a conversation that stays on a single topic and to ask questions, all in a non-threatening setting. During and after storytelling, provide children with plenty of opportunities to talk. Respond to what they say, evaluating their contributions positively and giving information about the meaning of words. Children need to feel confident about the whole reading experience and develop the foundations to become independent readers themselves.

Please see further tips for using the Pippa and Pop Big Books below.

**Reading tips**

*Before reading*
- Storytime should be a special, fun part of the class. If possible, have a 'story corner' with cushions or a mat for the children to sit on while you're reading a story (or sit on a chair and encourage the children to sit in a circle on the floor around you). Before reading, make sure everyone is sitting comfortably.
- You can use the Pippa puppet to introduce and help tell the story.
- Create a sense of anticipation by showing objects or pictures of things that appear in the story.
- Work with the first page and ask questions: What's this? What can you see?
- Help the children develop the skill of predicting by spending time looking at and talking about the pictures in the Big Book. It should be a collaborative activity: invite the children to come and point to the pictures and really interact with the book.
- Ask prediction questions, such as: What can you see? What do you think happens in this book? Who is this? What do you think happens to (name/object)?
- Note down the children's predictions for the story so that you can compare them with what actually happens while you are reading.
- Get the children to identify the setting and characters in the story before they listen.
- Use the Big Book pictures, actions and your drawings to pre-teach key vocabulary. This helps the children to enjoy the story, rather than feeling anxious about unknown words.

*While reading*
- Try to make reading a shared experience. If appropriate in your classroom, invite the children to help you turn the pages of the Big Book and point to the pictures. Praise them for following the story successfully.
- Encourage participation by using actions and sound effects or by having the children join in with repeated words and phrases.
- Go back to the children's predictions that you noted down in the Before reading stage and see how they compare with what is actually happening in the story.
- You can read the stories using different voices to make it clear who is speaking. Much of the meaning of the stories is communicated through your gestures and the pictures. Don't worry that there are some words that the children may not know. The important thing is to make the story sound as dramatic as possible.
- When you name a character or a familiar object, point to the image on the page to help relate the spoken word to the image. Once children become more familiar with the story, before naming a character or object, pause slightly to allow the children to say the word or join you in saying it.
- Take your time telling the story and ask the children lots of questions (see some suggested questions below and also in the Teacher's Book).
- Ask how different characters feel and why they are acting in a certain way. Encourage the children to make connections using their observations and experience of the world. This helps young learners develop the skills of inference and empathy.

*After reading*
- As the children become more confident, ask what the children think the story means, and how they can relate it to their own lives. Encourage them to think about the value from the story and how different characters demonstrate or don't demonstrate that value.
- Provide the children with opportunities to talk about the story and encourage them to extend their ideas. Respond to their suggestions and evaluate their contributions positively. They are learning the communicative skill of talking about a single topic, as well as becoming invested in the story.
- You may want to repeat the story several times over the course of a week. As the children become more familiar with it, they will join in with some of the language naturally.
- Children can also interpret the story in a multi-sensory way by acting it out, practising the language in controlled role-plays or doing a related craft activity. These are effective methods for helping young learners to process a story and demonstrate comprehension.
